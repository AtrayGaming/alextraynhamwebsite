01Opportunity & audience

Guests form expectations before they arrive.

My original paper asked how theme park content on social platforms shapes expectations. Working in attractions made that question feel practical: people arrive with an idea of the experience, then have to make decisions in a busy, unfamiliar setting.

How can content build excitement while helping someone understand what to expect?

The audience for the recommendations was a theme park marketing or guest experience team. The objective was to examine the relationship between promotional content, anticipation, and trust, then identify questions worth investigating further.

02Research & limits

What the research shows, and what it does not.

I combined a literature review with a student-targeted survey using scaled, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions. The three respondents studied hospitality/theme park management, theatre, and entertainment management. All reported visiting parks at least four times per year.

What the paper reports

All three selected ride previews and behind-the-scenes footage among appealing content types. Trust ratings for promotional materials ranged from 2 to 4 on a five-point scale.

What that cannot establish

This small convenience sample cannot represent park audiences or prove content causes dissatisfaction. None of the three reported that their actual visit was disappointing.

My interpretation now

The original paper makes some broad recommendations. With three respondents, I would frame those as ideas for further testing. The useful next question is whether a realistic, decision-focused preview helps a particular audience understand an experience without reducing its appeal.

That distinction matters to my development in analytics: a finding can be interesting and still be too limited to justify a broad claim.

03Operations perspective

What operations work taught me about explaining things.

At Epic Universe, I helped commission and open attractions, then became an Opening Trainer at Constellation Carousel and Curse of the Werewolf. The work is explaining new things to people who are new to them: running multi-day training, coaching new team members through to sign-off, and answering questions until the material makes sense.

I also build the materials my venues teach with: a laminated stroller pass produced with Guest Services, voided Attraction Assistance Pass samples, regular and gold, sourced with the Express team, and a radio-codes curriculum I created (a quick-match activity and flashcard set).

I see a clear connection to content: establish the context, make the next step understandable, and check whether the audience took away what you intended.

This is a high-level professional reflection. No internal training materials, procedures, incident details, or guest records are reproduced.

04Original work sample

A planning sample written for guests.

To apply that thinking, I developed a short planning-content sample for a mixed-preference group. This is new hypothetical portfolio work, informed by the question above rather than presented as a finding from the survey.

Draft web or social planning card

Before you pick your next stop

Start with one thing each person wants from the day. A ride? Time to explore? A shared meal?

Choose a moment to enjoy together and agree on where to meet if you split up. Check current attraction information before making the plan.

Different choices can still be part of the same day.

Why this wording

It starts with a decision the guest can make. The examples are concrete. It does not assume everyone wants the same experience or promise fixed timing.

How I would adapt it

Use the full version on a planning page. Turn each question into a short carousel slide. Pair a captioned vertical edit with a link to current official information.

05Evaluation & reflection

How I would test whether it works.

I would show the draft to five adults planning a group visit, ask them to describe the next step in their own words, and record where the wording becomes unclear. A practical first revision gate is four of five understanding the takeaway. That would be a usability check, not a representative research result.

What I would do differently next time

Recruit beyond classmates, define the target segment before collecting responses, preserve anonymized raw results, and separate exploratory observations from claims that require a larger study. I would also test an actual content sample rather than asking only general questions about promotional media.

Completed: the academic paper, this methodological reflection, and a draft communication sample. Still to do: audience testing and a documented revision based on what people say or do.
