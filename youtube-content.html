03Creative decisions

Make a clear promise. Then deliver on it.

  1. Choose a specific idea. Identify the person who would care and what they should get from watching. For a cover, the recognizable song and performance format are the promise.
  2. Package that promise. Let the title explain what the video is and use the thumbnail to make the subject readable at a small size. The two should work together.
  3. Plan the opening. Review how quickly the actual content begins. An opening should earn attention before asking viewers for anything.
  4. Publish and listen. Read comments for questions and reactions, then compare them with whatever performance data is available. A strong comment is useful context, not representative audience research.

Packaging exercise: the published title

Original title

“Hello” By OMFG Piano Cover

The song, artist, and format are explicit. That gives a viewer a clear expectation. Without traffic-source and impression data, I cannot attribute discovery or watch time to this title.

Alternative A

OMFG – Hello | Piano Cover

A proposed order that leads with artist and track. Test whether it is easier to recognize in a music-search context.

Alternative B

Hello on Piano | OMFG Cover

A proposed order that leads with the performance format. Test whether viewers correctly anticipate the content.

Untested copy studies. Neither alternative is presented as a published change or an improvement in performance.

What I would test next

Hold the title fixed and compare a clear performance frame with a tighter instrument-focused thumbnail, using owned frames from the video. Ask a small group what each promises before any channel test. Avoid changing the title, thumbnail, and opening at the same time.

04Audience analysis

A useful question comes before a dashboard.

I enjoy looking at YouTube Analytics because it raises questions about creative decisions. For this portfolio, the next step is a documented comparison with consistent time windows, not a collection of impressive-looking numbers.

Proposed review · Private channel data still needed
Question Evidence to collect Decision it could inform
Does the packaging earn a relevant click? Impressions and impression click-through rate, split by traffic source where available Whether to investigate the topic, title, thumbnail, or distribution mix
Does the opening match the promise? Retention at 0:30 and the opening retention curve Whether to shorten setup or make the content clear sooner
Where does attention change? Average view duration, average percentage viewed, and dips or spikes Which moments need a closer editorial review; the curve alone does not prove why
What reaches the intended audience? Traffic sources, search terms where available, new / returning viewer context, comments Which follow-up ideas may be relevant
Is there value beyond the first upload? Longer-term discovery, repeat questions, usable excerpts, production hours Whether an evergreen follow-up or adapted version is worth the effort

A fair comparison

Start with three comparable long-form uploads. Use the first 28 days after publication and record runtime, topic, date, traffic sources, and any promotion. Keep Shorts and live streams separate. Older uploads may not have the same reporting available; record missing data instead of filling it in.

Any thumbnail experiment needs a documented method. A sequential change can be affected by timing and traffic mix, so it would not establish a causal lift on its own. New review findings belong here only after the data has been collected.

YouTube's engagement reporting definitions provide the reference for average view duration and retention. The proposed analysis is my own.

05Reflection

What I can bring to a content team now.

Independent creation taught me ownership: an idea needs a finished edit, a clear title, a thumbnail, and a reason to exist for an audience. It also made me interested in the gap between what a creator intends and how viewers respond.

For branded content, I would bring that practical experience into a more structured brief: audience, objective, approvals, distribution, and a measurement plan agreed on before publishing. Those are the strategic skills I'm continuing to develop.
