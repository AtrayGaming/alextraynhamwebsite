Academic case study · Group project

Marketing Panem like a real place.

How Lionsgate turned a sequel launch into a living story world, and what our UCF case study found when we took the campaign apart piece by piece.

Academic group project. Created with Tyler Schnittker and Jeremiah Robinson for a UCF course and presented in October 2025. I researched and presented the opening sections: the case overview, the marketing challenge, and the digital brand storytelling framework. The full 17-slide deck is linked at the end of this page.

My role
Research and presentation: case overview, marketing challenge, and the storytelling framework
Subject
Lionsgate's marketing campaign for The Hunger Games: Catching Fire (2013)
Format
Slide deck and written case study, presented in class

Academic · October 2025

What this demonstrates: campaign analysis, transmedia strategy, channel architecture, and evaluation that names the weak points as well as the wins.

The challengeThe frameworkThe channelsEvaluation
01The challenge

A sequel with a different problem.

The first Hunger Games film earned nearly $700 million worldwide. Just as important, it built an online fan community that was already creating fan art, running pages, and analyzing every trailer frame. The internet had effectively become an extension of the story world before the sequel's campaign began.

So Catching Fire did not have an awareness problem. It had a harder one: keep that fandom engaged for another film without making the marketing feel repetitive, and do it with a smaller team and budget than Disney or Warner Bros. could bring to a franchise launch.

The objectives our case study identified were simple to say and hard to do: blend traditional promotion, the trailers and posters, with immersive digital experiences, and make audiences feel like participants in Panem rather than passive viewers. The breakthrough idea underneath the whole campaign was to market Panem like a real place.

02The framework

Storytelling where the audience has a role.

The framework I presented starts with a definition. Digital brand storytelling uses connected content across platforms to tell one cohesive story that builds emotion and identity. The audience is not just watching; they interact, respond, and help spread the story. The goal is participation, not impressions.

That makes it different from traditional campaign thinking, where one trailer gets posted to YouTube and reshared everywhere with identical messaging. In a transmedia approach, each platform tells a different part of the story, and the world keeps existing between releases.

The Four Ps of transmedia storytelling

Our case study evaluated the campaign against four qualities:

  • Persistent. The story continued online between films. TheCapitol.PN updated from 2012 to 2015 with new content for each release.
  • Pervasive. The campaign met fans where they already were, with in-universe content on Tumblr, Instagram, Twitter, and YouTube, carried by hashtags like #CapitolCouture and #OnePanem.
  • Participatory. Fans could share, comment, reblog, and create their own Capitol posts, becoming part of the world instead of an audience for it.
  • Personalized. Through TheCapitol.PN, fans received district assignments and digital citizen IDs, with Capitol-approved updates addressed to them.
03The channels

Each platform did a different job.

Capitol Couture (Tumblr) was the hub: an in-world fashion magazine covering the Capitol as if it were real, which gave fans a cultural space inside the story rather than ads about it. It anchored subcampaigns including Capitol Portraits, Game Faces, District Heroes, and Victory Tour Posters, released incrementally to build anticipation.

TheCapitol.PN registered fans as citizens of Panem, complete with district IDs. YouTube hosted the trailers alongside in-world propaganda, including President Snow's Panem Address videos framed as real broadcasts. Twitter and Instagram carried in-character posts and fan reblogs that kept expanding the universe between official drops.

The point our group kept returning to: none of these channels repeated the others. Each one added a piece of the world the others did not have.

04Evaluation

What worked, what limited it, what I took from it.

The group's verdict was that the campaign was bold, immersive, and visually distinctive, and that it deepened fan engagement in a way standard sequel marketing rarely attempts. The strongest evidence: Lionsgate did not abandon the approach after Catching Fire. For Mockingjay Part 1, the same ecosystem carried forward, and Capitol Couture flipped from a luxury fashion magazine into a propaganda outlet as the story darkened. The marketing followed the plot instead of resetting.

We also named the limits. Tumblr and Twitter skewed toward younger fans, so reach had a ceiling. The fully in-world format could confuse new or casual audiences who had no guide into it. And updates across platforms sometimes felt sporadic, leaving gaps in the story. Our recommendations addressed each one: a central hub connecting every element, recap videos or explainers to onboard new fans, a consistent release schedule, and real-world collaborations to widen the audience. Rebuilt for 2025, we argued the same campaign would lead with short-form video, interactive district filters, and creator partnerships.

The lesson I kept is the one I now bring to my own work: market the world, not just the release, and give the audience a role in it. That is the thinking behind my channel, and behind the Universal concept elsewhere on this site.
